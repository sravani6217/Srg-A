Sagittarius A* is the supermassive black hole at the centre of our Milky Way galaxy. Its gravity affects the motion of nearby stars, gas and light.

01 / Meet Sagittarius A*

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity becomes so strong that nothing can escape from inside its event horizon, not even light.

LIVE VISUALIZATION · NOT TO SCALE

What is it?

A supermassive black hole containing approximately four million solar masses.

Where is it?

At the gravitational centre of our Milky Way galaxy.

Can we see it?

Not directly. We observe its shadow and the emission from surrounding hot matter.

What are you looking at?

Dark centre: The black-hole shadow is a dark region in the image, not a visible surface.

Bright ring: Emission from hot gas, shaped partly by gravitational lensing.

Rotating disk: Matter orbiting the black hole. It is not the event horizon itself.

02 / Anatomy of a Black Hole

Explore the important regions around a black hole.

1. Singularity

The central region predicted by classical General Relativity where the theory's description becomes singular. A complete quantum-gravity description is still unknown.

2. Event Horizon

The boundary beyond which no future-directed signal can escape to distant observers.

3. Photon Sphere

For a non-rotating black hole, light can travel on circular photon orbits at 1.5 times the Schwarzschild radius. These orbits are unstable.

4. Accretion Disk

Hot gas and plasma orbiting the black hole. Friction and other processes heat the material, producing radiation.

5. Black-Hole Shadow

The dark image feature formed by light capture and gravitational lensing. It is larger than the event horizon.

6. Surrounding Space

Objects can orbit, pass by, or fall towards the black hole depending on their speed and trajectory.

Important: The photon sphere and event horizon are different. The accretion disk is outside the event horizon.

03 / Einstein's General Relativity

Gravity is not simply a pulling force. In General Relativity, mass and energy curve spacetime, and objects follow paths through this curved geometry.

CONCEPTUAL SPACETIME GRID

What changes near a black hole?

Space

Distances and the geometry of space are affected by mass.

Time

Clocks closer to a massive object run slower relative to clocks far away.

Motion

Objects and light follow paths determined by curved spacetime.

Einstein Field Equation Gμν = 8πG Tμν / c⁴

Geometry of spacetime is related to the distribution of matter and energy.

Why is this used in our project?

We use General Relativity to explain why the motion of objects and the paths of light change near Sagittarius A*. A flat-space model cannot fully describe these effects.

04 / Gravitational Lensing

Light normally travels along straight paths in flat spacetime. Near a black hole, curved spacetime changes its path.

Distance: 5 Rs

What happens to light?

1. Light approaches the black hole.

2. Its trajectory bends because spacetime is curved.

3. Depending on its impact parameter and direction, it may escape, orbit temporarily, or be captured.

4. Light that crosses the event horizon cannot escape back out.

This is a qualitative 2D ray illustration. Exact light trajectories require solving the null geodesic equations.

Possible outcomes

Stable orbit: An object with suitable angular momentum can orbit the black hole.

Deflection: An object passing nearby may change direction.

Accretion: Gas can lose energy and angular momentum and spiral inward.

Capture: An object on a captured trajectory crosses the event horizon.

06 / Time Dilation & Gravitational Redshift

Gravitational Time Dilation

A clock closer to a non-rotating black hole runs slower relative to a clock far away, when compared by distant observers.

Schwarzschild Clock Formula dτ / dt = √(1 - Rs/r)

dτ = time measured by a stationary local clock

dt = time measured by a distant observer

Rs = Schwarzschild radius

r = distance from the black-hole centre

5.00 Rs

Relative clock rate:

0.894

The nearby stationary clock runs at about 89.4% of the distant clock's rate.

Gravitational Redshift

Light travelling outward from a strong gravitational field loses energy and is observed at a lower frequency, or longer wavelength.

Gravitational Redshift Formula f∞ / femit = √(1 - Rs/r)

The closer the emission point is to the event horizon, the greater the gravitational redshift for a stationary emitter.

These formulas describe stationary observers outside an ideal non-rotating Schwarzschild black hole. A stationary observer cannot remain at the event horizon.

Schwarzschild Theory

Karl Schwarzschild found an exact solution to Einstein's field equations for the spacetime outside a spherical, non-rotating, uncharged mass. It provides the simplest mathematical model of a black hole.

Schwarzschild Radius Rs = 2GM / c²

G = gravitational constant

M = mass of the black hole

c = speed of light

For Sagittarius A*, the Schwarzschild radius is approximately 12 million kilometres.

What about rotation?

A rotating black hole is described more accurately by the Kerr solution. Rotation changes spacetime around the black hole and produces an effect called frame dragging.

The accretion disk rotates around Sagittarius A*. Do not confuse its rotation with the event horizon behaving like a solid spinning surface.
